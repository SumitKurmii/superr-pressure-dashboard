How pressure is defined

Two models learn from the data: a win-probability model (chases AUC 0.89) and a leverage index, the win-probability gap between the next ball being a six and being a wicket, divided by the league average. A pressure ball has leverage of at least 1.5 and win probability between 10% and 90%. That covers 17.9% of all balls and 29% of chase balls.

How stable are the lists?

Names kept from the final top 10 when one assumption changes. Batters hold up better (6 to 9 of 10) than bowlers (4 to 7 of 10); places 7 to 10 are close calls.

Method in brief

  • Each ball gets a run value: runs plus the change in expected remaining runs, so wickets and wasted balls count.
  • Players are compared with their own usual level and with the league in the same match state. Small samples are shrunk toward the average (empirical Bayes).
  • Ranking uses the 5th percentile of 1,000 resamples of whole matches.
  • Eligibility: at least 60 pressure balls (batters) or 48 (bowlers) in 8+ innings, and at or above league average in the player's own pressure sample.
  • 53 matches involving associate nations are excluded.

Limitations

  • Pressure and the 1.5 leverage threshold are analyst choices; places 7 to 10 move under other settings.
  • First-innings win probability is weak, so first-innings pressure is under-identified.
  • No adjustment for opposition, venue, season or batting order beyond match state and format.
  • Pressure-specific skill beyond general quality is small, so intervals matter more than exact order. Split-half rank correlation is about 0.30.
  • Sooryavanshi's lead rests on 92 balls; the model deliberately pulls it toward the mean.
  • Rankings describe past performance only, not availability, fitness, price or squad fit.
